**Q:** I'm eight months into my first TPM job, at a warehouse software company. Our VP
promised our biggest customer a November 15th launch on a sales call with no engineers
present. The work needs three teams, and one of them is booked through December.

I showed him the capacity numbers, and he agreed the date won't happen. He still wants me to
show the project as green at the customer's quarterly review next Thursday, because their
contract renews in January. His words were "green means we're working on it."

I don't want to lose this job, and I don't want to tell a customer something I know is false.
What do I do on Thursday?

*Green in Name Only*

---

**A:**

GiNO, I don’t envy your position and you’ll have some hard choices to make, but this won’t be the last time you have to do this so the good news is the first time is usually the worst time and it gets better after that. I’m fairly certain just about every product manager, owner, and technical product manager has been in your shoes. Going to the data is always a solid step for two reasons. First, it checks your own biases to make sure what you think is true is true. Second, it’s evidence for your claim so your argument to the VP is sound. That said, it’s not a great leadership sign when commitments are made without consulting the people doing the work. I want to caveat all this advice with, you know your context best. You know the people you work with, your company, customers, and personal situation better than anyone else.

One thing you have to have really solid is your values. Where are you not willing to compromise? It sounds like being dishonest or deceptive with the customer is something that you will not flex on. I’d love to tell you everything works out fairly but the reality is that there are a good number of companies who do not appreciate people who push back. If you think pushing back might cost you your job, that’s something to consider. I’m going to go forward with the assumption that you are ok with giving pushback. That is pretty core to what TPMs do.

There are a few things I notice about what you said that are worth talking about. All of these should be reconciled before you get on the call with the customer. It’s possible the VP already has a plan to resolve the tension of the team being booked and the deadline presented to the customer. Ask, “Hey I want to be supportive in this endeavor, is there something you have planned that you haven’t let me in on yet? The data shows we can’t do it and I’m not comfortable deceiving the customer. Help me understand your perspective and plan.”

For the project status, it sounds like as a company there might not be a shared definition of what “green” status means. Your VP is saying that ”working on it” means it can be green. If “working on it” means green, how are red and yellow defined? Theoretically, the “working on it” definition means every project will always be green, so what’s the value of the status color in that case?

If for some reason, you can’t agree with your VP on a unified message that aligns to your values you have two options: follow orders or tell the customer what you know. It’s possible to do so tactfully without raising major alarms usually, but not always. Either way, you have to make the best decision for your context. You can report what is factual. You can say what’s been completed, what has yet to be done, and which things you’re most worried about. Don’t offer up a date, but if you’re asked directly, give a clear answer and make sure that it’s clear you’re giving the estimate based on what you know at that moment. Always caveat with the fact that it can change.
