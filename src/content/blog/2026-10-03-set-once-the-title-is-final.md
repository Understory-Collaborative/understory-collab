I remember being in a meeting with a product manager, and we were talking about tradeoffs. She said, "I'm going to push for everything, even though I know it's too much. It's my job to push for more than you can deliver, and your job to push back." To that, I ask, why? We're both knowledgeable, skilled professionals. Why can't we work in the same direction to deliver value for the business and our customers instead of expending energy arguing?

When you know the shape of someone's thinking, you can tailor the conversation, and the whole team can row in the same direction.

## The pull between roles keeps a product balanced

I think the existence of these different roles is, in some ways, a tension that exists to keep things in balance. If everyone were a product manager, then the long-term health of the product would never be prioritized.

The roles I work with most are product managers, tech leads, dev managers, and architects, and they tend to differ in two ways. The first is time, and the second is who they can talk to.

Product managers tend to be good about either long-term vision or immediate need, but it's rare that I find a product manager who can balance the two. Architects lean toward long-term vision and the long-term health of the product, so it's really hard to convince an architect to short shrift it. Tech leads do a pretty good job of balancing short-term versus long-term health. Dev managers tend to be more stop-the-bleeding, immediate, reactive people. They're firefighters, or maybe it's just that most of the tech leads I've come in contact with are not really firefighters. Leadership, of course, tends to be the pie in the sky. All they really ever do is dream about things that they want without thinking about what it looks like to make that plan a reality. _\[Open: the firefighter sentence compares dev managers to tech leads mid-thought; cut the second half or say what you meant.\]_

Tech leads, architects, and dev managers tend to be amazing at technical knowledge and at technical communication to technical people. They need a nudge to be able to communicate with non-technical people.

## Deadlocks come from how we talk

When product wants something now and the architect won't cut the corner, the underlying problem is usually a balance of resources. Still, no one could argue that if product came in understanding that architects tend to be people who want to prioritize the long-term health of the product over short-term gains, they might be able to tailor their conversation better.

The push-for-everything stance from that meeting is the easy way out. One of my frustrations is that we assume there has to be a forced tension. I don't think there has to be a forced tension if we're all authentic about rowing in the same direction with regard to prioritization.

Neil Mercer, an education researcher who studies how people think together through talk, describes three ways a group can talk.

-   **Disputational talk** is assertions and counter-assertions, where each person restates their position and nobody takes up the other's reasoning.
-   **Cumulative talk** is agreeing and building on each other without questioning, polite but not thinking together.
-   **Exploratory talk** is engaging critically but constructively with each other's ideas: challenges come with a "because," people ask "what if," and the decision comes out of the group.

That product manager's "my job is to push for more, your job is to push back" stance is disputational talk. With exploratory talk, we assume we're working toward the same goal. The version where I want my way and we have to struggle against each other is the disputational one. That's in fact probably a lot of the reason people get into deadlocks, because we're not regularly acting like a team.

## Knowing someone's shape helps you tailor the conversation

The understory crew was talking about what it means to be a technical product manager (TPM): what problems we solve, and what the differences are between managers and owners, and between product, program, and project. What we came to was that they're a family of roles based on the same skill set, just in different shapes, and the family includes architects, staff engineers, and tech leads. Combining that with research on teamwork competencies and metacognition, which is how well you notice your own thinking, we developed the TPM archetypes.

The shared skill set has six parts:

-   **Tactical:** the right-now call that stops the bleeding.
-   **Technical:** deciding the tradeoffs without a translator.
-   **Creativity:** improvising a path when the standard one is blocked.
-   **Strategic:** where it needs to be in three years, worked backward.
-   **Communication:** adapting to whoever's in front of you, so they leave knowing what's true.
-   **Leadership and collaboration:** getting a group to its best work, disagreement included.

In order to visualize this, we made each of those qualities a point on a hexagon. Archetypes are named for the two skills where that archetype is strongest.

In archetype terms:

-   Product managers often read as **Navigators** (strategic and communication).
-   Architects often read as **Architects** (technical and strategic).
-   Dev managers often read as **Firefighters** (tactical and creativity).
-   Leadership often reads as **Visionaries** (creativity and strategic).
-   Tech leads often read as **Captains** (tactical and strategic).

Just because we've used these examples with these archetypes doesn't necessarily mean every dev manager you meet is a Firefighter. The key is "often read as."

Drawing from the research of Carol Dweck, the Stanford psychologist known for her work on growth mindset, none of these qualities is immutable. These are archetypes we may shift into and out of across our careers in technology.

Every archetype has a **complement** that is strong in the areas where it is weak. For example, the Firefighter archetype is weakest in strategic thinking, which is why its complement is the Navigator, who is strongest in strategic and communication. A **polymath perspective** is having at least a little of every shape, enough to understand the pull of the others. If everybody had a little bit of the other archetypes, we'd move toward at least understanding a polymath perspective.

In my last role, at one point while we were working on a transition, my manager asked me to think about how I spend my week as a thought experiment and to document it. At first I expected I wouldn't be able to offer any meaningful feedback on his experiment. I knew what I did, and I'd been a diligent time tracker for years, so what surprises could possibly be revealed? I did it anyway, because it was, after all, still a hypothesis.

When I did, I was surprised by what I found. I tracked my time and found myself naturally grouping the things I viewed as product owner work separately from the things I viewed as technical product manager work.

I think something many organizations struggle with is making transition time a reality. It's easy to know what now looks like and what you want it to look like eventually, but the key is knowing when eventually happens and what things look like between now and eventually. By the end of the experiment, I was able to more meaningfully convey which aspects of my role I wanted to move away from and which ones I wanted to make sure were the focus of my days.
