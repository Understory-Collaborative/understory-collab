When a project keeps reporting green and isn't, there are really only two reasons. Either the team is missing a skill they can learn, or they're missing safety that only the leader can build. Fix the right one, and people bring problems to you first, while there's still time to solve them.

In [The project is 90% done for six weeks in a row](/blog/90-percent-done), I wrote about the questions a leader can ask to get a straight answer. This post picks up where that one stops, with what we do once a leader hires Understory to help. I'll walk through the signs you can spot on your own, how we tell which gap you're dealing with, and what we do about each.

## The signs you can see

You don't need to be in the weeds to notice these:

-   People who used to speak up in meetings have gone quiet.
-   Status meetings turn into haggling over the color.
-   The date moves, and the status stays green.

A team will usually tell you a project is in danger, right up until they've gone unheard enough times that they stop saying anything. Sometimes it’s another stakeholder in the room not listening to the team’s warning signs or trying to talk them out of what they’re reporting.

Sometimes you, the leader, put on earmuffs and pretend that what the team is saying isn't being said. It might not even be intentional. Your mind is on something else or you're the eternal optimist. When they go quiet, it can look calm, and it's often the opposite.

In our fire stages, we call this a [smolder](/field-guides/smolder.pdf), where the team knows and leadership doesn't. By the time someone who always spoke up gives notice, you're past a smolder and into [crown fire](/field-guides/crown-fire.pdf) territory.

## How we tell which gap it is

We start with what the leader who hired us tells us. Patterns from past work point us to the likely problems, and we treat those as guesses to prove or disprove, so we diagnose your problem instead of the most common one. We check the numbers, and we watch and listen to people, because numbers alone won't tell you why.

Next, we talk to the full team. We tell them why we're there and how things will go. We keep disruption low, and we don't play the blame game. We're looking for the underlying reason the project isn't delivering on time.

Then we interview a few people chosen for the problem at hand. We listen to how the team talks about the project, look at how documents are structured and who sees them, and notice whether people feel free to share their work. We also ask for examples of how people work together, including how leadership works with the team.

## If it's a skill gap

A skill gap means the team doesn't yet have the language or the framework to report the real state of the project. It covers more than it sounds like:

-   If the work isn't visible yet, the team should be able to say the work is invisible. Hidden work that only shows up late is [one of the two causes researchers found](https://calhoun.nps.edu/entities/publication/6021b259-851a-4bdc-a265-33d31ee92161) behind projects that stall near the end. Saying "this part is invisible" is a skill.
-   If nobody defined what green means, someone on the team needs to be able to call that out. Calling out missing criteria is a skill.
-   If the team truly believes they're almost done, that's optimism, and estimating under uncertainty is a skill.

We start by sitting next to the person who reports status and watching how they do it in meetings. Then we ask where they struggle, and where the right words for the problem don't come.

I did this with a product owner I used to work with. She was struggling to explain her vibes on how a project was doing, so I shared the [confidence score](/blog/cant-fix-a-delivery-by-changing-status) with her. It gave her specific areas to talk about. She could say, "This is a high-complexity project, so it's tanking our confidence that we can get it done on time," or "The requirements aren't clear, and we need more information from product management on these three things." She felt more confident, and the stakeholders and the team could finally have a productive conversation. That conversation brought in more people to help, and the project was delivered on time without the date shifting again.

## If it's a safety gap

A safety gap means the team knows the real state and doesn't feel safe saying it. That happens when red status gets punished, and it happens when leadership doesn't hear the bad news it's given, which researchers call the [deaf effect](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/the-deaf-effect-response-to-bad-news-reporting-in-information-sys-3/).

We don't play the blame game with leaders either. This is the part of the fix only you can make.

Here, the first step is for the leader to observe their own behavior. We sit in on a meeting with them, and afterward we talk about how they reacted to certain things. Everyone is a bit different, so we don't offer a recommendation until we've seen them in the mix and talked to their people. The first stage is listening, to the leader and to the people who report to them.

Listening means more than hearing the feedback. I once gathered feedback from the people who reported to a leadership group and built the leaders a playbook from it. They never acted on it, and they never told their people they'd heard them. Even a simple "We got your feedback, and we're working out what to do with it" would have kept the conversation open.

It also means treating a problem someone brings you as a request for partnership. "Come to me with solutions, not problems" sounds efficient, but when someone brings you a problem, it's because they can't solve it alone. Work out the solution together, and you build their capacity and trust at the same time. As a leader, you're responsible for the culture of your organization, and listening to your people is where it starts.

Here's what it looks like when a leader listens. Another leader I worked with, let's call him Jim, took that advice. He acknowledged the feedback and told people he was working through it. He focused on being timely and showing progress, and people really valued the transparent updates. They were forgiving and gave him a pretty wide margin for error, as long as he kept showing he was reading and listening to the feedback. The key was that he came to those conversations with sincerity. If you aren't sincere in your appreciation of your people, they'll know. Soon people were bringing issues to him first.

Jim also treated problems as partnership. During an AI rollout, someone on the team, let's call them Sam, told Jim nobody was clear on what AI was for, where to share wins and struggles, or who to ask for help. He didn't make the fix Sam's homework, which is a fast way to teach people to stop raising problems. He asked what role Sam wanted to play. They didn't want to lead it, but they did want to collaborate, so Jim found someone who had been looking for a leadership growth opportunity. Together all three of them gathered input and built one place to find who to ask and what to do, with weekly check-ins for feedback, struggles, and wins. Sharing the problem paid off more than once. The rollout got one source of truth, a teammate got a chance to lead, Sam stayed willing to raise the next issue, and Jim didn't have to solve it alone. The hard part was not falling back on "come to me with solutions" and working through the human side of the problem instead, which takes nuance and some vulnerability from the leader.

## Where to start

Once we find the underlying reason, we support the team until they're successful. For a skill gap, that means coaching the person who reports status until the new words are their own. For a safety gap, it means working alongside the leader until listening is a habit, not an effort. We know it's time to leave by comparing how things were when we started to how they are now. When the gaps have closed, we stop seeing the behaviors and habits that brought us in. Then we Mary Poppins out of there, because the goal is for them not to need us anymore.
